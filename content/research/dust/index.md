Cosmic dust — tiny solid grains made of heavy elements like silicon and carbon — is one of the most influential components of the interstellar medium. Despite accounting for only about 1% of the interstellar mass, dust has a profound impact on galaxy evolution. It absorbs and scatters ultraviolet and optical light, reprocessing it into the infrared; it catalyzes the formation of molecular hydrogen on grain surfaces; and it regulates the thermal balance of the gas by shielding dense clouds from energetic photons — controlling where and how efficiently stars can form.

Dust grains are born in the final stages of stellar evolution: in the winds of evolved asymptotic giant branch stars and in the ejecta of core-collapse supernovae. Once released into the interstellar medium, they evolve in both mass and size through a competition of processes — growth via accretion of metals from the gas phase and grain-grain coagulation, versus destruction by supernova-driven shock waves and energetic radiation. The balance of these processes determines the total dust mass of a galaxy and the **grain size distribution** — a quantity that controls nearly all of its observable dust properties.

My research develops and tests physically motivated models for all of these processes, implementing them as sub-grid prescriptions within both hydrodynamic simulations and semi-analytic models. I then confront these models with a wide range of observational constraints — dust masses, dust-to-gas ratios, infrared spectral energy distributions, and dust temperatures across a broad range of galaxy types and redshifts — in order to both understand the observations and improve the current state of dust and ISM modeling.

A central focus of my current work is understanding how the grain size distribution evolves with cosmic time, what drives its variation across galaxy populations, and what observable imprints it leaves — from the shape of the infrared SED to the efficiency of PAH formation and emission. This connects directly to observations with Herschel, ALMA, and JWST, which are now probing dust properties in galaxies across the full history of the universe.
