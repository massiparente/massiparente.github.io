What do we really see when we observe galaxies through telescopes? Translating what cosmological simulations predict into quantities that telescopes can actually observe is a central aspect of my research.

A large fraction of star formation across cosmic history takes place inside dust-enshrouded regions that are nearly invisible at optical and ultraviolet wavelengths. To study these galaxies, we rely on their infrared emission — the radiation that dust and gas re-emit after absorbing the light of young stars. The infrared sky is therefore a unique and indispensable window onto galaxy evolution. I model three complementary probes of this emission.

**Far-infrared continuum.** The thermal emission of dust grains peaks between roughly 50 and 200 μm in the rest-frame spectrum, producing the characteristic modified blackbody of star-forming galaxies. The shape and luminosity of this spectral energy distribution encodes the dust mass, temperature, and optical depth of the galaxy. By linking the physical properties of dust in simulations — grain sizes, composition, spatial distribution relative to stars — to the emergent spectrum, I build a physical picture of what the infrared SED really reveals about the galaxies that produce it.

**Far-infrared fine-structure lines.** The interstellar medium emits brightly in atomic fine-structure transitions in the far-infrared. Lines such as [CII] 158 μm, [OI] 63 μm, and [OIII] 88 μm are among the most luminous features in star-forming galaxies and have become primary diagnostics of ISM conditions in the ALMA era, providing information on gas density, ionization state, metallicity, and the local UV radiation field. I model these lines by coupling the physical state of the gas in cosmological simulations with photoionization and photodissociation region codes, producing synthetic luminosity functions and line maps for direct comparison with high-redshift ALMA observations.

**Mid-infrared PAH features.** This is the frontier I am most actively working on right now. Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons emit a distinctive set of broad features across the mid-infrared — at 6.2, 7.7, 8.6, 11.3 μm and beyond — that are among the brightest tracers of star-forming regions in the universe. Despite their observational ubiquity, PAH emission remains poorly understood theoretically, and models capable of predicting PAH features from first principles within cosmological simulations are still in their infancy. I am developing such models, with the goal of establishing PAH emission as a powerful and independent probe of galaxy evolution. This work is directly motivated by upcoming facilities like [PRIMA](https://prima.ipac.caltech.edu/), a proposed NASA mission that would revolutionize our view of the mid-infrared universe and for which PAH modeling will be a key scientific driver.

Together, the continuum, fine-structure lines, and PAH features provide a rich, multi-dimensional view of the ISM in galaxies across cosmic time — one that I use to test theoretical models and help interpret the extraordinary datasets now emerging from ALMA and JWST, and those that future observatories will deliver.
