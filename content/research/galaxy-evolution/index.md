How do galaxies form, grow, and eventually quench their star formation across cosmic time? This is one of the central open questions of modern astrophysics, and one I tackle through large-scale cosmological simulations and semi-analytic models (SAMs).

Galaxies are not isolated objects: they assemble their stellar mass through a complex interplay of gas accretion, star formation, feedback from stellar winds and supernovae, and the growth of central supermassive black holes. Understanding how these processes balance and compete — and how their relative importance shifts across cosmic time — is at the core of galaxy formation theory.

My work spans two complementary approaches. In **hydrodynamic simulations**, I follow the coupled dynamics of gas, stars, and dark matter self-consistently, enabling detailed modeling of the multiphase interstellar medium. In **semi-analytic models**, I can efficiently explore large parameter spaces and make statistical predictions across a broad range of galaxy masses and environments. I am particularly active in the latter approach in the context of [L-Galaxies](https://lgalaxiespublicrelease.github.io/), a state-of-the-art SAM for which I have developed a dedicated branch with an advanced treatment of dust mass, grain size distributions, and PAH evolution.

Galaxy evolution is a highly interconnected problem: the many physical processes involved are deeply entangled and difficult to disentangle in isolation. My research has therefore spanned several of its aspects — from the role of supermassive black holes and large-scale structure to the mechanisms driving galaxy quenching. The common thread running through all of this work is using **dust and dust-driven observables** as a window into the physical processes that govern how galaxies evolve.
